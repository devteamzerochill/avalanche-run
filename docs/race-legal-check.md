# Multiplayer race: legal check (2026-11-24)

Done without a lawyer, at the owner's request. This is a research summary of public sources, not legal advice. It covers the United States in most detail, because the site's Terms are under Wyoming law and most of the public material is American; other countries are only flagged.

## Bottom line

**As designed, the race carries real gambling risk in parts of the US, and it conflicts with zerochill.com's own Terms.** The game itself isn't the problem: it is skill-based. The risk comes from what goes in and what comes out:

- **Players pay to enter** with Shards, and Shards **can be bought with real money**.
- **The winner gets Shards**, which have value (they can be bought, and they unlock items).
- **The pot is funded only by entry fees**, and since 2026-11-24 the house **takes a 10% cut** of every pot.

Gambling law in most places needs three things together: **consideration** (paying to play), **chance**, and a **prize**. The race has the first and third. Whether it is legal then rests entirely on the game counting as "skill, not chance", and that test differs from state to state.

## What the site says about Shards

From zerochill.com/terms and /trust, read 2026-11-24:

- Shards are a virtual item used only inside the service, with **"no cash value"**. They can't be cashed out, sold, transferred between players, or redeemed for money or crypto.
- **Players can buy Shards** (Terms section 5), with a first-purchase doubling bonus.
- Section 6: **"No purchase is ever needed to enter an event or win a prize"**, and buying Shards never improves the chance of winning one. Section 3: Zero Chill games "are games of skill".
- Accounts are 13+; under-18s need a parent's permission to buy anything.
- "You may not use the Service where it is not allowed by local law. Some features, such as prizes, may not be available in every country." No country list is given.
- The Trust page's "Regulatory matters" section says "Statement in preparation" (empty).

**Conflict:** a race that costs Shards to enter, where Shards can be bought, is an event with a purchase needed to enter. That contradicts section 6 as written.

## The three elements, applied

| Element | The race | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Consideration | Entry fee in Shards; Shards are sold for money | **Present.** Paying with a virtual currency that is sold for money is generally treated like paying money. |
| Prize | 90% of the pot in Shards | **Likely present.** Shards can't be cashed out, which helps, but a federal appeals court held that virtual chips that only extend play were a "thing of value" under Washington law (*Kater v. Churchill Downs*, 9th Cir. 2018), and the Washington Gambling Commission says later cases followed it. Shards unlock cosmetics and gifts, so they are at least as valuable as those chips. |
| Chance | Same seed and slope for every rider; the result comes from steering, timing and endurance | **Low, but it varies by state.** Most states use a "dominant factor" test (skill must outweigh chance), which this game should pass. Some states ask whether chance is a material element at all, or count any element of chance. Network lag and the server's tie-breaking rules could be argued as chance. |

## Where it is riskiest (US)

Public sources disagree on details, so treat this as a list to verify, not a final map:

- **States usually listed as restricting paid-entry skill contests:** South Carolina (even skill games with entry fees), Arkansas, Delaware, Louisiana, Arizona, and sometimes Tennessee and Montana. Skill-gaming companies such as Skillz exclude several of these.
- **Pots funded only by entry fees:** some states (Arizona and Florida are named) may treat a tournament whose prize pool comes only from the entrants as gambling. Our pot is exactly that.
- **Washington:** besides *Kater*, Washington is strict about online games for prizes.
- **The 10% house cut:** taking a share of each pot is how gambling operators earn money (a "rake"). Burning the 10% gave the house nothing from the pot; paying it to a house account does. Regulators can treat that as the operator profiting from players' stakes. This is the main effect of the house-fee change on legal risk.

## Outside the US (not checked in depth)

The site doesn't limit countries. Many countries regulate paid-entry prize competitions, often more strictly than the US. Not researched: the UK, the EU, Australia, the Philippines and others where players may be. Check before allowing real Shards in any country with many players.

## Ways to bring the risk down

These are options for the owner and the website team to choose from, not decisions:

1. **Free entry.** No entry fee; the prize comes from the house (for example a fixed Shards prize per race). With no consideration, the race isn't gambling in most places. This matches the Terms as they are.
2. **Pay only with earned Shards.** If the site can keep earned Shards separate from bought ones, entry fees could take only Shards earned by playing. That is weaker than option 1 (they still have value), but there is no purchase.
3. **A free way in.** Let every player enter for free a set number of times a day, so the paid entry is optional. This is the "no purchase necessary" model the site's Terms describe for events.
4. **Drop the house cut** (back to burning, or a 100% payout) if paid entry stays. This removes the rake argument.
5. **Block restricted places and ages.** No entry from the states listed above, and 18+ only for paid races, as the site already requires for mining and crypto rewards.
6. **Write official rules** for the race (eligibility, how winners are decided, ties, refunds, the house fee) and link them from the race panel. Sweepstakes and contest laws usually require these.
7. **Fix the Terms** (website team): either change section 6 or make the race fit it.

## What this check didn't do

- No lawyer and no regulator was consulted. State laws, attorney general opinions and recent cases were not read in full; the state list comes from secondary sources that disagree in places.
- Didn't read the site's event rules, refund policy, or the mining and Honest Money terms.
- Didn't check how players in each country actually buy Shards (prices, payment providers), which can bring in their own rules.
